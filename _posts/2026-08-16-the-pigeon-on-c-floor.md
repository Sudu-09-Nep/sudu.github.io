**Opening**
He watched a pigeon chick try to fly from a hostel window. What he didn't expect was that the chick would teach him something about himself.

He lived on C Floor of the hostel, which was, confusingly enough, the second floor.

A few steps from his room stood a drinking-water tap. Beside it was an elevator which, as far as he could tell, had moods of its own. It worked when no one needed it and refused to move whenever the hostel needed the most.

The outer wall of the elevator shaft projected slightly into the quadrangle, leaving a narrow recess beside a window. It was in that recess that he first noticed the pigeon's nest.

He could not remember exactly when it had appeared. Pigeons were in the hostel on the room and sometimes in the lawns. Ordinarily, he paid no attention to them. But this nest was only a few steps from his door, and without making any deliberate decision, he began to follow its affairs.

There were two chicks in it at first.

One of them died.

The other survived.

To him, the surviving chick was a peculiar-looking creature. Its feathers did not yet resemble proper feathers. They stuck out from its body like little thorns, exposing patches of tender pink skin. Its head appeared too large for its body, and its feet seemed to belong to a much older bird.

Whenever he passed the recess, he looked at the nest.

Sometimes he saw the mother feeding the chick. At other times, he found the chick alone, sitting quietly in the nest and staring at something he could not see.

A few boys in the hostel also took an interest. One would crush a biscuit and scatter the crumbs near the nest. Another would stand at a distance making affectionate but foolish sounds, as though the chick understood human language but had chosen not to reply.

Whenever he watched, the chick appeared to do very little.

It ate.

It slept.

Sometimes, for no reason he could understand, it shook its entire body. The few feathers it possessed trembled violently, and then it became still again.

He found this entertaining.

The hostel provided very little entertainment during certain hours of the day, and the chick gradually became one of his regular programmes.

That evening, however, he had almost forgotten it.

A storm seemed to be preparing itself. During the evening-snacks break, he stood beside the elevator with a cup of tea in his hand and watched the sky.

"Wah," he thought. "What a beauty!"

The sun appeared to be making one last attempt to rule the evening. But thick black clouds had gathered above the hostel and were steadily occupying the sky. Here and there, sunlight escaped through gaps in them and fell upon the earth in pale, slanting patches.

To him, the sky looked like a blue canvas across which someone had carelessly thrown black ink.

Far in the distance, on the hilltop, stood the sixty-five-foot statue of Lord Hanuman. From where he stood, even Hanuman appeared to be waiting to see what the weather would do.

The clouds were heavy with rain.

The sun was stubborn.

The wind was impatient.

And he, holding a cup of tea, had nothing better to do than watch them quarrel.

"Let us see who wins today," he thought.

Below, a few boys were moving about the ground. Some appeared to be playing games. Others might have been returning from their evening duties. From the second floor, they looked like small figures moving inside a large painting.

A flock of birds crossed the sky.

He followed them with his eyes.

They must be returning to their nests, he thought.

Then he remembered the pigeon.

He walked towards the recess and looked inside.

The chick was alone.

Where had its mother gone?

Perhaps she was among the birds crossing the sky. Or perhaps she had gone in search of food. He could not tell. What he could see was that the chick was behaving differently that evening.

It came slowly to the edge of the nest.

It spread its wings.

It bent its legs.

For a moment, he thought it was about to jump.

But it did not.

It went back into the nest.

He smiled.

A few moments later, the chick came forward again.

It opened its wings, bent its legs and looked down into the quadrangle.

Again it retreated.

He waited to see what it would do next.

The chick tried again.

It came forward.

It opened its wings.

It bent its legs.

And once again, it went back.

"What a silly creature," he thought. "If you want to fly, fly. If you don't want to fly, sit quietly."

The chick did neither.

It stood inside the nest for a while. Then it returned to the edge and looked down.

He was beginning to lose patience with it.

"What is the use of coming forward if you are only going to go back?" he muttered.

"Exactly," said a voice.

He turned.

The corridor was empty.

The doors of the rooms were closed. The elevator stood with its usual expression of indifference.

He looked towards the staircase.

No one was there.

"Who said that?" he called.

There was no answer.

He looked at the chick again.

It was still standing at the edge.

"What a stupid creature," the voice said. "Every moment it makes a decision, and the next moment it abandons it."

This time, the voice appeared to come from somewhere very close to him.

Another voice laughed.

"It wants to be happy but somehow manages to remain sad."

He frowned.

"One moment it believes it can do everything," said the first voice.

"The next moment it cannot do anything," replied the other.

"It makes great plans when nobody is watching."

"But when the time comes to act, it forgets everything."

"One moment it is confident."

"The next moment it fumbles with every word."

"Cheerful now, depressed five minutes later."

"It changes its mood faster than the weather."

"Ha! Mood swings at their peak!"

"What the hell?"

He shouted and turned around.

There was no one.

His voice travelled along the corridor and came back to him as an echo. One of the doors moved slightly in the wind, but nobody stepped out.

He walked to the parapet and looked down.

The boys who had been visible on the ground were gone.

Had they returned to their rooms? Had they gone to the temple? Had the approaching rain driven them away?

He did not know.

The hill was still there.

The statue of Hanuman was still there.

The clouds were still resisting the sun.

Only the small human figures had disappeared.

He lowered his eyes and noticed the empty cup in his hand. He could not remember when he had finished the tea.

The voices had been speaking about the pigeon.

At least, that was what he had believed.

But a pigeon did not worry about happiness. It did not fumble with words. It did not wake with confidence and lose it before evening. It did not examine its moods and become ashamed of them.

Those words were not about the pigeon.

They were about him.

He had heard them before. They had spoken inside his room, before he met other people, after he made mistakes and during those long hours when his mind found fault with everything he had done.

He had never heard them so clearly.

The voices had not come from the corridor.

They had come from somewhere within him.

For a moment, he forgot the pigeon, the storm and the empty cup.

If the voices were his, why did they speak against him?

If he was speaking, who was listening?

And if he was listening, who was passing judgment?

He looked at the chick again.

It stood at the edge of the nest with its wings half-open.

Until then, he had believed that he was watching the creature. Now he wondered whether all its approaches and retreats had been showing him something about himself.

Who was watching whom?

Nothing outside appeared to have changed.

The sun still struggled behind the clouds. The wind moved through the quadrangle. The statue of Hanuman remained upon the distant hill, watching the uncertain evening.

The chick opened its wings once more.

It bent its legs.

Then it stepped back into the nest.

Earlier, he would have laughed at it.

Now he could not.

The first drop of rain fell into his empty cup. Across the quadrangle, the clouds slowly swallowed what remained of the sun.

The mocking voices had fallen silent.

From somewhere beneath that silence, another voice arose. It was quieter than the others, but he heard it clearly.

"Do not fight the voices," it said. "Follow them to their source. The moment you know where they arise, they cease to rule you."
